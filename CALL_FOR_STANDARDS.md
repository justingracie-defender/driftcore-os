# A CALL FOR AI & ROBOTICS SAFETY STANDARDS

*An open invitation to governments, universities, colleges, industry,
and independent makers.*

---

## The Short Version

Machines that can act in the world — software agents, and especially
robots — are arriving in homes, workplaces, and public life faster than
the standards meant to keep them safe. Right now, much of that safety is
improvised, proprietary, or simply absent.

This is a call to fix that, together, in the open:

1. **Governments** — establish baseline safety standards for AI systems
   that can cause real-world harm, the way we already do for cars,
   elevators, electrical work, and aircraft.

2. **Universities and colleges** — create training and certification
   pathways for the people who will build, inspect, operate, and repair
   these systems. The qualified people this future needs do not yet
   exist in the numbers it will require.

3. **Everyone** — treat safety infrastructure as shared public good, not
   a competitive advantage to be hoarded. A safer ecosystem benefits all
   of us, including the companies in it.

DriftCore OS is offered as one open, working starting point — not the
answer, but evidence that a principled, auditable, human-centered safety
layer is buildable today. Take it, challenge it, improve it, replace it.
What matters is that the standards get built.

---

## Why Now

We are at the moment cars were at before seatbelts, traffic laws, and
driver's licenses. The technology is here. The accidents are beginning.
The rules have not caught up.

Recent real-world failures show the pattern: AI systems deployed into
high-stakes, customer-facing, or physical roles **with no human in the
loop, no drift detection, no audit trail, and no clear accountability.**
When something goes wrong, the failure compounds silently — sometimes
for months — and when the damage surfaces, there is often no one clearly
responsible because the system was designed with no one watching.

This is not an argument against AI or robotics. It is an argument for
doing them the way we eventually learned to do every other powerful
technology: **with standards, training, accountability, and the humility
to assume imperfection.**

---

## What We Are Asking Governments To Consider

We do not presume to write law. We ask that the following principles be
considered as the basis for real, enforceable standards:

- **A human stays accountable.** Any system that can cause serious or
  irreversible harm must have a clear, identifiable human or institution
  responsible for it, and human oversight that cannot simply be switched
  off for convenience.

- **Severity-scaled requirements.** A chatbot and an industrial robot
  should not face identical rules. Standards should scale with a system's
  capacity to cause irreversible harm — distinguishing software-only
  systems from those that can physically affect the world.

- **Auditability by default.** Systems in consequential roles should keep
  tamper-evident records of their decisions, so that when something goes
  wrong, investigators — and the public — can see what happened and why.

- **Reachable safe paths.** Safety requirements must be satisfiable by
  the people who actually deploy these systems, including small operators
  and individual builders. A standard no one can meet is one everyone
  ignores.

- **No autonomous lethal decisions.** Decisions to harm should never be
  fully delegated to a machine. This is the clearest line, and it should
  be drawn in law, not left to each company's discretion.

---

## What We Are Asking Universities and Colleges To Build

The coming decades will need a workforce that does not yet exist:

- **AI Safety Engineering** — degree tracks and certificates covering
  drift detection, oversight design, interpretability, red-teaming, and
  failure recovery as core engineering disciplines, not electives.

- **Robotics Safety Technician programs** — community-college and trade
  pathways producing the people who will inspect, certify, and safely
  restart physical systems in the field. This is the "qualified
  technician" that safety standards will require — and who barely exists
  today.

- **Short, accessible certifications** — online courses, on the scale of
  a food-handler's card or a boating license, that teach an operator the
  difference between *safe* and *unsafe to restart*. Not everyone needs a
  degree; everyone operating one of these systems needs the basics.

- **Open curricula** — teaching materials shared freely across
  institutions, so that a school in a small town and a university in a
  capital city teach the same safety fundamentals.

The goal is a future where "I am qualified to inspect this robot" means
something specific, learnable, and available to ordinary people — not a
credential locked inside a handful of corporations.

---

## What We Are Asking Industry

- Treat safety as **shared infrastructure**, not a moat. The company that
  hoards safety technology while others ship unsafe systems is not safer
  — it lives in the same ecosystem and inherits the same public backlash
  when accidents happen.

- Support **interoperable, auditable standards** even when they constrain
  you, because the alternative — a patchwork of incompatible proprietary
  safety claims no one can verify — serves no one, including you.

- **Fund the training pipeline.** The technicians and safety engineers you
  will need won't exist unless someone helps the schools build the
  programs. That someone should include you.

---

## What Independent Makers Are Owed

The people building robots in garages and small workshops are not the
problem to be regulated away. They are often the source of the
innovation, and they deserve a real path to safety — not exclusion.

Any standard worth adopting must include a route by which an individual
builder, with no factory behind them, can be held to the **same standard
of responsibility** as a corporation — proven through demonstrated
competence, accepted responsibility, peer review, and honest record,
rather than through institutional gatekeeping alone.

If the standards lock out the makers, the makers will route around the
standards, and everyone will be less safe for it. Include them.

---

## DriftCore's Place In This

DriftCore OS is a small, open, working example — one attempt to encode
these principles into something that actually runs: immutable safety
invariants, drift and sycophancy detection, graded autonomy, human
oversight that cannot be disabled, tamper-evident audit trails, hardware
interlocks, severity-scaled restart authority, and a path for individual
builders.

It is not certified. It is not finished. It is not the standard.

It is proof that the standard is *possible* — and an open invitation to
the institutions who can make it real.

Fork it. Test it. Break it. Cite it. Ignore it and build something
better. What matters is not this project. What matters is that, this
time, we build the seatbelts *before* the crashes teach us to.

---

## How To Engage

- **Researchers and educators:** open an issue or discussion on the
  repository proposing curricula, evidence, or critiques.
- **Policymakers and standards bodies:** this document and the project
  are free to cite, adapt, and build upon under the project's license.
- **Makers and engineers:** contribute code, red-team the system, and
  share what real deployment teaches you.
- **Everyone:** the strongest message is not "we built another AI." It is
  *"we can build a safety and governance layer that sits between humans,
  machines, and the powerful systems shaping our future — and we can do
  it in the open, for everyone."*

This is the future. Let's make it a safe one, together.

---

*This is a call for collaboration, not a claim of endorsement. DriftCore
OS is an independent open-source project. No government, university, or
company named or unnamed has endorsed it. That is precisely what this
document asks to change.*
